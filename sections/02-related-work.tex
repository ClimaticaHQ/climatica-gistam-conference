\subsection{Existing Climate Visualisation Tools}\label{sec:related:existing-tools}
Several web tools offer access to the climate data visualization, ranging from simple city profiles
for general audiences to advanced platforms for climate researchers. 
Table~\ref{tab:existing-tools} summarizes their main features.

\subsubsection{Climate-Charts.net}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:climate-charts}
ClimateCharts.net~\cite{zepner2021climatecharts} generates Walter--Lieth climographs
for locations worldwide and is designed for a broad audience. However, it describes
one location at a time and offers neither comparison between places nor analysis
over a region.

\subsubsection{Climate-Data.org}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:climate-data}
Climate-Data.org~\cite{climatedataorg} presents climate tables and climographs for
cities, mainly oriented towards travellers. Its information is limited to individual
locations, with no comparison between periods and no spatial view.

\subsubsection{Meteoblue}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:meteoblue}
Meteoblue~\cite{meteoblue} provides simulated climate diagrams and historical weather
derived from reanalysis data. Several of its advanced history features, however,
require registration or a paid subscription.

\subsubsection{NASA Giovanni}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:nasa-giovanni}
NASA Giovanni~\cite{acker2022using} is a web platform for analysing NASA satellite
data that can produce time-averaged maps and time series. Access requires an
Earthdata account, and the interface assumes familiarity with remote sensing datasets.

\subsubsection{Copernicus C3S}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:copernicus-c3s}
The Copernicus Climate Data Store~\cite{buontempo2022copernicus} gives access to
ERA5 reanalysis and climate projections, along with tools for custom processing.
Using it requires registration and a level of technical knowledge that most
non-specialists lack.

In summary, tools aimed at the general public are limited to single-location,
station-based views, whereas tools offering spatial and temporal analysis target
expert users. None of them combines global gridded data, comparison across
locations and periods, and regional analysis in an interface suitable for
non-specialists, which is the gap addressed by \webappname{}.

\subsection{WorldClim}\label{sec:related:worldclim} % ±half a page
Weather stations alone are distributed unevenly across the globe, so their records alone cannot provide
a complete picture of climate for all areas. WorldClim~\cite{worldclim} addresses this issue by estimated climate
value for every point of the land surface. In Version~2.1, Fick and Hijmans~\cite{fick2017worldclim} retrieved 
climate data by interpolating station observations with the help of covariates such as elevation, distance to
the coast and satellite measurements. It is published as a set of grids with 4 resolutions 10 arc-minutes 
($\approx$18.5\,km at the equator), 5 arc-minutes ($\approx$9.2\,km), 2.5 arc-minutes ($\approx$4.6\,km), and 30
arc-seconds ($\approx$900\,m). Thanks to its global coverage and fine resolution, WorldClim has become a common
data source in species distribution modelling~\cite{jimenez2021climate}, climate
change impact studies~\cite{berio2023examining} and crop suitability
analysis~\cite{xian2022predicting}.

For the reference period 1970--2000, the dataset offers monthly values of minimum, mean and maximum temperature, 
precipitation, solar radiation, wind speed and vapour pressure, (and 19 bioclimatic variables more), computed from 
temperature and precipitation. A second collection contains month-by-month weather for 1951--2024,
built upon CRU-TS~\cite{harris2020version}. Averaging these records over 30-year windows
yields four additional climate normals (1951--1980, 1961--1990, 1981--2010 and
1991--2020)~\cite{scrapi}, so five periods are available to examine how climate has
changed over time.

The main obstacle is the format. The data come as thousands of GeoTIFF files
(8{,}836, about 85.6\,GB in total), and even a simple question such as the average
July temperature of a city requires locating the right file and extracting the value
with GIS tools or libraries such as GDAL~\cite{mcinerney2015open}. Comparing periods
or summarising a whole region multiplies this effort, which explains why the dataset
is rarely used outside the research community.

\subsection{Running Example}\label{sec:web-application:running-example}

To illustrate the capabilities of \webappname{}, we use a task from forest
management practice proposed by \climateexpert{}. Forest management plans
typically include an assessment of the local climate over the preceding 30-year
period, which guides decisions such as which tree species are suitable for a site.
As the climate shifts, a manager updating such a plan needs to know not only the
current climate of the site but also how it has changed and how it varies across
the managed area.

Consider a forest manager updating the plan for a stand near \examplecity{}.
The manager needs to:
(T1)~obtain the climate profile of the site for the most recent normal (1991--2020);
(T2)~quantify how the climate has changed relative to an earlier normal (1961--1990);
(T3)~examine how temperature varies across the management unit; and
(T4)~compare the site with an alternative location that may serve as a climate analogue for future conditions.

With raw WorldClim data, each of these tasks would require locating the relevant
raster files, downloading them and processing them with GIS tools.
Section~\ref{sec:web-application:features} shows how \webappname{} can perform all of these tasks
easily without requirements of guidelines or any GIS expertise.
