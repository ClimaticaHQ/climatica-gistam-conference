\subsection{WorldClim}\label{sec:related:worldclim}
Weather stations are distributed unevenly across the globe, so their records alone
cannot provide a complete picture of the climate in all areas. Reanalyses and satellite
products offer complete coverage, but at relatively coarse resolutions of tens of
kilometres. WorldClim~\cite{worldclim} takes a different approach: in version~2.1, Fick
and Hijmans~\cite{fick2017worldclim} interpolated station observations with the help of
covariates such as elevation, distance to the coast and satellite measurements, producing
estimates for every point of the land surface. The data are published as grids at four
resolutions: 10 arc-minutes ($\approx$18.5\,km at the equator), 5 arc-minutes
($\approx$9.2\,km), 2.5 arc-minutes ($\approx$4.6\,km) and 30 arc-seconds
($\approx$900\,m). Thanks to its global coverage and fine resolution, WorldClim has become
a common data source in species distribution modelling~\cite{jimenez2021climate}, climate
change impact studies~\cite{berio2023examining} and crop suitability
analysis~\cite{xian2022predicting}.

WorldClim offers several monthly variables, including minimum, mean and maximum
temperature and precipitation, as 30-year averages for the reference period 1970--2000.
In addition, it provides monthly weather data for each year from 1951 to 2024, derived
from CRU~TS~\cite{harris2020version}, a widely used dataset built from records of thousands
of weather stations, and refined to the WorldClim resolutions down to 2.5 arc-minutes.
Averaging these yearly data over 30-year windows yields four additional climate normals
(1951--1980, 1961--1990, 1981--2010 and 1991--2020)~\cite{scrapi}, so five periods are
available to examine how climate has changed over time.

As a data archive, WorldClim is well organised, thoroughly documented and uses an
open format (GeoTIFF), which makes it convenient for researchers. For other users,
however, its size is a barrier: it comprises 8{,}836 files, about 85.6\,GB in total, and
answering even a simple question requires locating the right file and extracting values
with GIS tools or libraries such as GDAL~\cite{mcinerney2015open}, as illustrated in
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. Comparing periods or locations multiplies this effort.

\subsection{Existing Climate Visualisation Tools}\label{sec:related:existing-tools}
Several web tools give access to climate information, ranging from simple location
profiles for the general public to advanced platforms for climate researchers. All of 
them cover the whole globe, but they differ in how users select a location, what they 
can compare, how results can be exported and shared, and how rich the underlying data are.
Table~\ref{tab:existing-tools} summarises these differences, which are discussed
below for each tool.

\subsubsection{ClimateCharts.net}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:climate-charts}
ClimateCharts.net~\cite{zepner2021climatecharts} is the tool closest to \webappname{}.
It generates Walter--Lieth climographs, distribution charts and data tables for
user-defined periods since the early twentieth century, using station records from
GHCN~\cite{lawrimore2011ghcnm} and three gridded datasets, including
CRU TS~\cite{harris2020version}. Charts can be downloaded as PNG or SVG images,
although the underlying data cannot be exported. However, all gridded datasets have a resolution of
30 arc-minutes, so a single cell spans about 55\,km at the equator and local differences,
such as those between a valley and nearby mountains, are lost. With regard to usability,
locations can be selected only on the map or by coordinates, not by place name, and the
URL does not reflect the current chart, so it cannot be shared as a link. Finally, each
chart describes a single location and period, so comparisons require generating several
charts and contrasting them manually.

\subsubsection{Climate-Data.org}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:climate-data}
Climate-Data.org~\cite{climatedataorg} is mainly oriented towards travellers and presents
climate tables and climographs for cities, computed from ECMWF data at
0.1--0.25$^\circ$ for a single period (1991--2021). Users can search for cities by name, 
and each city has its own page that can be shared through its URL. Yet, the search does 
not accept coordinates, the site offers no way to compare locations or periods, and neither 
charts nor data can be exported.

\subsubsection{Meteoblue}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:meteoblue}
Meteoblue~\cite{meteoblue} is primarily a weather forecast service that also offers
climate diagrams. These are derived from 30 years of simulations of its own weather model
at about 30\,km resolution, while longer historical series, available since 1940, require
a paid subscription. Its location search is the most complete among the reviewed tools,
accepting both place names and coordinates and displaying candidate places on a map,
although it does not tolerate typing errors. Each location has its own URL, but
comparisons between locations or periods are limited, and charts can only be
downloaded as images.

\subsubsection{NASA Giovanni}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:nasa-giovanni}
NASA Giovanni~\cite{acker2022using} allows users to analyse more than 2{,}000 satellite
and reanalysis variables through time-averaged maps, time series and other plots, and
its URL preserves the selected options. Results can be exported as PNG images or as
GeoTIFF and NetCDF files, formats that require GIS software to open. 
However, regions are defined by bounding boxes
or predefined shapes such as countries rather than by place names, guest users are
limited to a few time steps, and full use requires an Earthdata account. Its interface
also assumes familiarity with remote sensing products, which places it out of reach of
most non-specialists.

\subsubsection{Copernicus Interactive Climate Atlas}\label{sec:related:existing-tools:c3s-atlas}
The Copernicus Interactive Climate Atlas~\cite{c3satlas} visualises datasets from the
Copernicus Climate Data Store, including reanalyses such as ERA5~\cite{hersbach2020era5}
and ERA5-Land~\cite{munoz2021era5} and global and regional climate projections, at
resolutions from 0.1$^\circ$ to 2$^\circ$. It covers a long time span, from
1940 for reanalyses to 2100 for projections, and offers time series, annual cycles and
climate stripes, values relative to reference periods or global warming levels, export
as CSV, PDF and PNG, and shareable links, without registration. However, climate
information is aggregated over predefined regions, such as IPCC reference regions or
countries, or over user-drawn polygons, so the climate of a specific city cannot be
looked up by name or point. The Atlas is designed primarily for policymakers and climate
service users, and its wealth of datasets, scenarios and options assumes familiarity
with climate science.

In summary, tools for the general public are easy to use but serve coarse data or a
single period and describe one location at a time, whereas platforms that support richer
analysis, such as Giovanni and the Climate Atlas, target expert users and aggregate data
over regions or require familiarity with remote sensing products. Moreover, none of the
tools aimed at the general public lets users choose among several spatial resolutions.
No existing tool combines fine-resolution global data spanning several decades,
comparison across locations and periods, place search, export and shareable views in an
interface suitable for non-specialists, which is the gap addressed by \webappname{}.
