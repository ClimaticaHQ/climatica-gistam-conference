Climate change has become one of the most pressing global concerns. According to the Peoples' 
Climate Vote 2024~\cite{flynn2024peoples}, 69\% of people worldwide report that climate 
change influences their major life decisions. Understanding how temperature,
precipitation or solar radiation evolve in a given region, and comparing climate across
periods or locations, can support informed decisions in agriculture, tourism, urban
planning and many other domains.

However, accessing climate data remains challenging for many users. The combination of large 
data volumes, multiple spatial and temporal scales, and specialized data formats often requires 
the use of advanced GIS software and technical expertise. 
WorldClim~\cite{worldclim}, for instance, is an open dataset offering global gridded
climate surfaces for several variables, such as temperature, precipitation and solar
radiation, at resolutions from 30 arc-seconds to 10 arc-minutes. Besides 30-year
climate normals, it also provides monthly weather data for every year from 1951 to 2024,
which makes it possible to study how climate has changed over recent decades.
Nevertheless, retrieving and visualising the information needed from WorldClim is
cumbersome: it requires downloading large raster files and processing them with
specialised GIS software, which demands time and expertise that most potential users
lack. Imagine, for instance, a user who wants to know the maximum temperature in Rome
during the extreme heat of summer 2003. They first have to find the relevant file among
the more than 8{,}000 published by WorldClim, each package being about 70 to 250 MB at 2.5 arc-minutes
resolution. They then need GIS software or a library such as
GDAL~\cite{mcinerney2015open} to read its contents and, not least, determine which
pixel corresponds to Rome. Only after these steps it could discover that the maximum
temperature in July and August 2003 was almost 3\,$^\circ$C above the 1970--2000 normal.

Existing web tools only partially address this problem. 
Climate-Charts.net~\cite{zepner2021climatecharts}, a representative example, generates 
Walter--Lieth and bars climographs from several global datasets for any location and a 
user-defined period. However, each chart describes a single location and period, and charts 
cannot be overlaid, so comparing the climate of different places, or of the same place across 
periods, requires generating separate charts and contrasting them manually.

Other applications aimed at the general public, such as Climate-Data.org~\cite{climatedataorg}
and Meteoblue~\cite{meteoblue}, share similar limitations, while platforms such as
NASA Giovanni~\cite{acker2022using} and the Copernicus Climate Data Store~\cite{buontempo2022copernicus}
support temporal and spatial analysis but require registration and specialised knowledge.
These tools also differ considerably in the data they serve, in terms of spatial
resolution, time span and the availability of both climate normals and yearly weather
data (see Section~\ref{sec:related}). WorldClim, by contrast, combines global gridded
coverage, several spatial resolutions and a long historical record. What it lacks is
an accessible interface that makes these data easy to explore for everyone who needs them.

% ? proposed solution
To bridge this gap, we present \webappname{}, a web application that provides
interactive access to the complete WorldClim archive without requiring GIS expertise.
The application retrieves data through the Semantic Configurable Raster API
(SCRAPI)~\cite{scrapi}, which also allows new data, such as future projections or
additional variables, to be incorporated without changes to the application.
Its main contributions are:
(i)~a typo-tolerant, multilingual search over more than 12~million populated places,
which also accepts geographic coordinates;
(ii)~visualisation of selected climate variables for any location worldwide, chosen
through the search or on an interactive map, as Walter--Lieth climographs or bar charts;
(iii)~comparison of climate across time periods and between locations; and
(iv)~export of results as images (PNG, SVG) or raw data (CSV, JSON) for further
research, and sharing of any view through its URL.
We also report a user evaluation with students from several European institutions,
which assessed the usability of \webappname{} with the System Usability Scale (SUS)
and collected qualitative feedback (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} introduces the WorldClim dataset
and describes existing climate visualisation tools. Section~\ref{sec:web-application} presents \webappname{},
covering its requirements, data integration, architecture and main features. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} describes
the user evaluation and its results, also covering some discussion questions. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} 
concludes the paper and outlines the future work.
